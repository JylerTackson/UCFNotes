##### **Task 1: Summary of the Main Claims**  
Nick Bostrom’s _How Long Before Superintelligence?_ argues that advances in hardware and software will likely make superhuman artificial intelligence possible within the first third of the 21st century. He defines “superintelligence” as an intellect far surpassing human capability in nearly every domain, including science, wisdom, and social skills. By extrapolating Moore’s Law, Bostrom suggests that computers could achieve the necessary processing power to simulate the human brain—estimated between 10^14 and 10^17 operations per second—sometime between 2004 and 2024. He explores bottom-up approaches to AI, where machines learn from experience much like children, and notes that neuroscience may uncover the brain’s learning algorithms within a few decades. Once human-level AI emerges, he argues, positive feedback loops in self-improvement and replication will rapidly propel it to superintelligence. Despite uncertainties, he concludes that economic, military, and societal incentives will strongly drive the pursuit of superintelligence, regardless of risks.

##### **Task 2: Discussion of the Claims**  
Bostrom’s claims were bold but not entirely unrealistic given the technological optimism of the late 1990s. His hardware predictions were strikingly accurate in some respects—supercomputers did reach performance levels comparable to his lower-bound estimates within the projected timeframe. However, his expectations for software progress and neuroscience turned out to be overly optimistic; while machine learning and neural networks advanced significantly, they did not yield human-level general intelligence by 2024. His foresight about recursive self-improvement and the profound societal incentives for AI research remain highly relevant today, even if timelines were too aggressive. Rather than hype, Bostrom framed his arguments as possibilities with significant uncertainty, and his emphasis on both technical feasibility and ethical risks has proven prescient. The article shows both the ambition and the humility of early superintelligence forecasting—technically grounded, but inevitably challenged by the unpredictable pace of breakthroughs.
